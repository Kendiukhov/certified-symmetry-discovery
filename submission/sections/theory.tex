\section{How small a violation can anyone certify?}
\label{sec:limits}

Two questions bound what any procedure can do. How small a tolerance is
achievable, and when can the observed region of state space stand in for the
target domain?

\subsection{A resolution limit}

Exact symmetry cannot be certified from noisy data: a system with zero defect
and one with a defect just above zero generate almost the same data. The
useful version of this statement is quantitative.

\begin{proposition}[Le~Cam bound on the certifiable tolerance]
\label{prop:lecam}
Consider model \eqref{eq:model} with fixed design. Let $\varphi \in \{0,1\}$ be
any rule for certifying a fixed generator $\xi$ at tolerance $\delta$ whose
false-certification rate is at most $\alpha$, i.e.\
$\Prob_\beta(\varphi = 1) \le \alpha$ whenever
$\defect_\nu(\xi;F_\beta) > \delta$. Then for any $\beta^0$ with
$\defect_\nu(\xi;F_{\beta^0}) = 0$,
\begin{equation}
  \Prob_{\beta^0}(\varphi = 1)
  \;\le\; \alpha \;+\; \frac{\delta\,\sqrt{N}\,\|M_\xi \beta^0\|}
                            {2\,\sigma\, s_{\max}(\xi)} \;+\; o(\delta),
  \qquad
  s_{\max}(\xi) = \max_{\Delta \neq 0}
    \frac{\|L_\xi \Delta\|}{\|F_\Delta\|_{\Ltwo{\mu}}} .
\end{equation}
Equivalently, attaining power $\gamma$ requires
$\delta \gtrsim 2\sigma\,s_{\max}(\xi)\,(\gamma-\alpha)\,/\,
(\|M_\xi\beta^0\|\sqrt{N})$.
\end{proposition}

The constants are computable for a given problem, so this is a numerical
benchmark rather than a rate statement. \Cref{sec:resolution} evaluates it:
\symcert's certified tolerance tracks the bound's $\sigma/\sqrt N$ scaling
over two decades of $N$ and three noise levels, exceeding it by a factor of
$8.1$ to $8.8$. Part of that gap is ours, since a calibration-targeting
bootstrap is $9$--$18\%$ narrower (\Cref{sec:tightness}); the rest is not
attributable without a matching construction: \Cref{prop:lecam} is a lower
bound obtained through Pinsker's inequality and a single two-point
alternative, and we do not claim it is attained. What the comparison does
establish is that the \emph{rate} is right: no procedure, however clever, can
improve on \symcert{} by more than a constant factor.

\subsection{The price of incomplete coverage}
\label{sec:coverage-theory}

Suppose the data occupy a region with distribution $\mu$ while the claim is
about $\nu$. Every bracket $[\xi_\theta, F_\beta]$ lies in a fixed
finite-dimensional space $\mathcal R$ of polynomial fields, so the worst-case
discrepancy between the two measures is a single generalised eigenvalue.

\begin{proposition}[Extrapolation factor]
\label{prop:kappa}
Let $G_\mu$ and $G_\nu$ be the Gram matrices of a basis of $\mathcal R$ under
$\mu$ and $\nu$, and set
$\kappa = \lambda_{\max}(G_\mu^{-1/2} G_\nu G_\mu^{-1/2})$ and
$\kappa' = \lambda_{\max}(G_\nu^{-1/2} G_\mu G_\nu^{-1/2})$. Then for every
$r \in \mathcal R$, $\|r\|^2_{\Ltwo{\nu}} \le \kappa\,\|r\|^2_{\Ltwo{\mu}}$,
with equality attained, and consequently
\begin{equation}
  \defect_\nu(\xi;F) \;\le\; \sqrt{\kappa\kappa'}\;\defect_\mu(\xi;F)
  \qquad\text{for every } \xi \in \mathcal V .
\end{equation}
Moreover $\kappa = \infty$ if and only if some nonzero $r \in \mathcal R$
vanishes $\mu$-almost everywhere, i.e.\ the data lie in the zero set of a
possible bracket.
\end{proposition}

As a \emph{diagnostic}, $\kappa$ and $\kappa'$ are computable from the observed
states alone, before any symmetry analysis, and
$\sqrt{\kappa\kappa'}\,\delta$ is the tolerance that a defect of $\delta$
measured on the data can actually support on the target domain. As an
\emph{explanation}: declaring a symmetry at tolerance $\delta$ from a defect
measured on the data requires some generator whose \emph{true} defect satisfies
$\defect_\nu \le \sqrt{\kappa\kappa'}\,\delta$. Writing $\defect^\star$ for the
smallest true defect in the candidate class that is not already within
tolerance, a spurious symmetry is possible only if
\begin{equation}
  \sqrt{\kappa\kappa'} \;\ge\; \defect^\star / \delta .
  \label{eq:kappacrit}
\end{equation}
This is a one-sided guarantee. Falling below the threshold \emph{proves} that no
spurious symmetry can occur; exceeding it is a warning rather than a prediction,
since the bound is worst-case over the residual space. \Cref{sec:coverage}
confirms both halves: no false certification anywhere below the threshold, and
false certifications on some but not all settings above it.

The infinite case is not exotic. A single trajectory of a planar system with a
limit cycle eventually lies on an algebraic curve, and every bracket divisible
by the curve's defining polynomial vanishes on the data. We observe exactly
this (\Cref{tab:trajectory}).

\subsection{Why noise alone does not manufacture symmetry}
\label{sec:noise-theory}

It is natural to expect that noise makes small singular values smaller. Under
model \eqref{eq:model} the opposite happens. Since $\E\|L_\theta \bhat\|^2 =
\|L_\theta\beta^\star\|^2 + \mathrm{tr}(L_\theta \Sigma L_\theta^{\!\top})$,
and likewise for the denominator, estimation error inflates \emph{both} parts
of \eqref{eq:ratio}; for a direction that is close to a true symmetry the
numerator is near zero, so its relative inflation is unbounded while the
denominator's is not. The plug-in defect of a true symmetry is therefore
biased upward and all directions are pulled toward a common noise-dominated
value. The practical consequence is that a fixed tolerance loses
\emph{detections} as the noise grows, at a rate that depends on $\sigma$, $N$
and the design, none of which the tolerance knows, rather than gaining false
ones. \Cref{sec:calibration} measures this over $32{,}000$ replications
spanning noise levels from $0.1$ to $1.0$ and sample sizes from $30$ to $400$
on four systems with no symmetry: the median smallest plug-in defect is
$1.01$, $1.09$, $1.27$ and $1.64$ times the true one at the four noise levels,
and in not one of the $32{,}000$ replications did it fall below $0.05$.

A second effect pulls the other way. The generator chosen to \emph{minimise}
the plug-in defect is selected for a small value, so its true defect tends to
exceed its plug-in value: the winner's curse. The two effects compete, and
which wins is system-dependent: at noise level $0.2$ and $N=100$ the mean of
$\defect_\nu(\hat\xi;\beta^\star) - \hat\defect(\hat\xi)$ is $+0.0150 \pm
0.0017$ on Van der Pol, $+0.0078 \pm 0.0004$ on Lotka--Volterra, $+0.0060 \pm
0.0021$ on Duffing and $-0.0073 \pm 0.0009$ on the asymmetric rigid body. Both
effects decay with $N$ (the Van der Pol gap falls to $0.0002 \pm 0.0002$ at $N
= 6400$), and neither is large. But a selection effect of either sign is
enough to invalidate a test evaluated on the data that chose the direction,
and \Cref{prop:simultaneous} removes the need to reason about it at all.

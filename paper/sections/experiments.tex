\subsection{Experimental design}
\label{sec:experiments}

Ten polynomial systems on $\R^2$ and $\R^3$ serve as the main benchmarks, four
with a nonzero symmetry algebra inside the affine candidate class and six with
none (Methods). Their symmetry algebras are exact rational nullspaces and the
true defect of any generator is available in closed form, so \textbf{ground
truth involves no tolerance and no sampling}, which matters because the
quantity being estimated is itself a near-degeneracy. Every method is scored
identically, by the rate of \emph{false certification} (the declared subspace
contains a generator whose true defect exceeds $\delta$), of \emph{detection}
(the declared dimension is at least the true one) and of \emph{recovery}
(detection, with the declared subspace containing the true algebra to within
$10^\circ$). The worst-setting rates of \cref{tab:calibration}, the zero rate
of \symcert{} and selected rates in the text are given with exact binomial
intervals for their Monte Carlo error; Methods bounds that error for every
other rate. Unless stated
otherwise $\delta = \alpha = 0.05$ and the region is the exact least-squares
ellipsoid.

The framework we build on does not prescribe a threshold for declaring a
symmetry (it establishes that the symmetries are a nullspace), so there is no
single baseline, and we sweep the threshold instead of picking one, judging
false certification at $\delta = 0.05$ throughout. We give the threshold rule
the advantage of the \emph{exact} target-domain Gram matrix, which isolates the
statistical question from the coverage effect studied separately. The methods
are (i) a fixed threshold on the plug-in defect, swept over $\tau \in [0.005,
0.4]$; (ii) the largest relative eigengap; (iii) a Wald rank test for the
nullspace dimension; (iv) a split-sample significance test that selects a
direction on one half and tests $H_0$: exact symmetry on the other, declaring a
symmetry when it fails to reject; (v) a Weyl-inequality certificate, the
natural singular-value perturbation answer; (vi) \symcert; (vii) \symcert{}
with sample splitting, which chooses the plug-in directions on one half of the
data and certifies them with the simultaneous certificate on the other
(Methods).

\symcert{} \emph{never certified a false symmetry.} In none of the $72{,}000$
replications did the certified subspace contain a generator whose true defect
exceeds the tolerance. With $300$ replications per setting this bounds the
false-certification rate of every setting by $0.010$ (one-sided $95\%$), and
simultaneously over all $240$ settings by $0.028$. The guarantee itself comes
from \Cref{prop:simultaneous}; the simulation checks the implementation and
shows how far below $\alpha$ the realised rate is.

\emph{None of the non-trivial thresholds we examined controls the error.} A
threshold of zero, or one so small that it never certifies, controls false
certification trivially and has no power; among the thresholds from $0.005$ to
$0.4$ on this grid, every one has a worst-setting rate of at least $0.63$
($95\%$ interval $0.58$ to $0.69$), even though the average over settings is as
low as $0.005$ for small thresholds. Lowering $\tau$ does not help: at $\tau =
0.005$ detection has already fallen to $0.42$. For every threshold up to $0.1$
the worst setting is the planar rotation at the highest noise level with $N =
100$, and for thresholds up to the tolerance the only settings above $\alpha$
are that system at that noise level with $N = 100$, $200$ and $400$ (rates
$0.63$, $0.34$ and $0.12$). There the threshold declares the right number of
generators, and the declared subspace even lies within $10^\circ$ of the true
algebra, so the error counts as a recovery; but the noise has tilted it just
enough to contain generators whose true defect exceeds the tolerance, because
the defect grows linearly with the tilt. The remaining errors at $\tau =
\delta$, at rates of at most $0.04$, also occur on symmetric systems at noise
levels $0.1$ and $0.3$, where a tilted subspace of too small a dimension can
contain such a generator as well. Thresholds above the tolerance fail more
simply: they certify, on systems without symmetry such as the Lorenz system,
directions whose defect lies between $\delta$ and $\tau$. Averaged over the
symmetric settings, recovery and detection therefore differ by less than
$0.002$ for every rule in \Cref{tab:calibration} except the split-sample test,
and not at all for \symcert{}. That test declares at most one direction, which
is counted as a detection whenever it is declared; it detects at a rate of
$0.84$ but recovers at only $0.22$, because a single direction can neither
contain a two- or three-dimensional algebra nor be relied on to point along a
one-dimensional one.

\emph{Valid tests are not safe either.} The rank test and the split-sample
significance test are correct level-$0.05$ tests of ``this is an exact
symmetry''. Used as discovery rules they reach worst-setting false-certification
rates of $1.00$ and $0.92$ (mean rate $0.070$ for each). This is the inverted
burden of proof made quantitative: their power is lowest exactly where the data
are least informative, and low power is read as evidence for symmetry.

\emph{The tolerance is a choice.} Repeating the comparison across $\delta \in
[0.01, 0.4]$ on a smaller grid (six of the systems, $N = 400$ and $3200$, noise
levels $0.03$ and $0.1$; \SFtolerance), \symcert{}'s worst-case error is zero
at every tolerance, and its detection rate on the three symmetric systems
climbs from $0.25$ at $\delta = 0.01$ to $1.00$ at $\delta = 0.2$: with data of
this quality, a user who will accept a $20\%$ relative defect can have both
error control and full detection, and one who insists on $1\%$ cannot. The grid
leaves out the small samples and the highest noise level, where detection at
$\delta = 0.05$ is lower (\cref{fig:power}a).

\emph{Validity is not free, and neither is it vacuous.} \symcert{} detects
less often than a threshold at the same tolerance ($0.485$ against $0.784$;
for both rules recovery equals detection). \Cref{fig:power}a shows
where the cost falls: at noise level $0.01$ detection reaches $1.00$ by
$N = 400$; at noise level $0.3$ it does not, because the data at that noise
level do not support the claim. The Weyl certificate shows the alternative
failure: valid, and never certifying anything.

\subsection{Noise, coverage, and the model class}
\label{sec:coverage}\label{sec:modelclass}

\Cref{fig:mechanisms} isolates the candidate mechanisms.

In the sparse-regression group the orange bar is the threshold rule on the
STLSQ fit and the blue bar is \symcert{} on the unpruned least-squares fit,

\label{fig:mechanisms}

\paragraph{Additive derivative noise.} In the calibration study, where the
model class is correct, the states exact and the data cover the target domain,
the threshold rule at the tolerance never certified falsely on a system without
symmetry. We push this hard: four systems with no symmetry (Van der Pol,
Lotka--Volterra, Duffing and the asymmetric rigid body, with smallest true
defects of $0.21$ to $0.29$, at least four times the tolerance), noise levels
$0.1$, $0.3$, $0.5$ and $1.0$, $N = 30$, $60$, $120$ and $400$, $500$
replications per cell, $32{,}000$ in all. On Van der Pol, Duffing and the rigid
body the median smallest plug-in defect rises with the noise, to between $2.0$
and $2.4$ times its true value at noise level $1.0$ and $N = 30$ (pooled over
systems and sample sizes, $1.01$, $1.09$, $1.27$ and $1.64$ times at the four
noise levels); on Lotka--Volterra it stays within $1.00$ to $1.14$ times, with
$31\%$ to $59\%$ of replications below the true value. The smallest value
observed, $0.066$ (Lotka--Volterra, true value $0.271$), was still above the
tolerance, which reflects the margin to $\delta$ rather than an upward bias;
the argument for an upward bias holds only for directions near a true
symmetry (\Cref{sec:limits}). A
second study on the same four systems, at noise level $0.2$ with $N$ doubling
from $100$ to $6400$ and $500$ replications per cell, measures the competing
selection effect, which at this noise level is small and decays with $N$: at
$N=100$ the mean of $\defect_\nu(\hat\xi;F_{\beta^\star}) -
\defect_\nu(\hat\xi;F_{\bhat})$ for the selected direction is $+0.0150 \pm
0.0017$ on Van der Pol, $+0.0078 \pm 0.0004$ on Lotka--Volterra, $+0.0060 \pm
0.0021$ on Duffing and $-0.0073 \pm 0.0009$ on the asymmetric rigid body (means
$\pm$ standard errors), and the Van der Pol gap falls to $0.0002 \pm 0.0002$ at
$N = 6400$. With fewer samples and more noise it is larger, up to $0.15$ on
average in the selected-generator study of \Cref{sec:tightness}.

A false symmetry requires the noise to carry the smallest defect down to
$\delta$, so a third study uses systems near the tolerance: broken-Hopf members
whose smallest true defect is $1.2$, $1.5$, $2$ or $4$ times $\delta$, and the
hardening Duffing oscillator of the state-noise study below ($0.102$), on
$[-1,1]^2$, with $N = 100$, $400$ and $1600$, noise levels $0.05$, $0.1$, $0.2$
and $0.3$ and $300$ replications per setting ($18{,}000$ in all). No generator
is within the tolerance, so every declaration is false. The threshold rule at
the tolerance declared one in up to $3.7\%$ of replications at $1.2\delta$ ($N
= 100$, noise level $0.05$; $95\%$ interval $1.8\%$ to $6.5\%$), at most
$0.7\%$ at $1.5\delta$, $0.3\%$ at $2\delta$ and on the hardening Duffing
oscillator, and never at $4\delta$; \symcert{} certified nothing in any of the
$18{,}000$ replications. The smallest plug-in defect fell below its true value
in up to $52\%$ of replications, and at high noise and small $N$ it was
inflated (median up to $3.7$ times the true value). Additive noise therefore
does create false symmetries near the tolerance, but rarely in these tests, and
only when the smallest true defect is within about a factor of two of $\delta$;
the dominant noise-driven failure of a threshold rule is the tilt of the
estimated algebra of a symmetric system.

\paragraph{Coverage.} We shrink the region the data occupy from the full target
box to a box of $0.15$ times the half-width, keeping $N=2000$ and noise level
$0.02$, and run the nullspace computation on the empirical measure of the data,
which is what one does when the sample is taken to stand for the domain of
interest. \Cref{fig:headline}b plots the false-certification rate against the
extrapolation bound $\sqrt{\kappa\kappa'}$ (mean over replications) divided by
the value $\defect^\star/\delta$ of \eqref{eq:kappacrit}. Of the $50$ settings,
$13$ fall below this threshold, where \Cref{prop:kappa} rules out, at
population level, declaring more generators than the true algebra has (any
declaration, for the six settings of systems without symmetry). In all $13$ the
observed rate is exactly zero; this is an observation, not a consequence, since
the bound concerns the noise-free field and does not exclude a tilted
declaration of the right dimension. Of the $37$ beyond the threshold, $14$ (six
on Van der Pol, five on Hopf and three on the symmetric top) do produce false
certifications, rising to a rate of $1.0$. \symcert{}, which measures the
defect on the target domain and inherits the design's uncertainty through
$\hat\Sigma$, never certifies falsely and instead reduces the dimension of its
certified subspace to zero as coverage degrades: on the Hopf system it
certifies the true one-dimensional algebra when the data cover the domain and
almost never once the data occupy less than half of it. On trajectory data
(\STtrajectory) a single trajectory of the Hopf system converges to the unit
circle, an algebraic curve, so some bracket vanishes on the data to within the
eigenvalue threshold and the computed extrapolation factor is infinite; single
trajectories of the Van der Pol and Lorenz systems, whose attractors are not
such curves, give large but finite factors ($34.9$ and $231.3$), eight Hopf
trajectories bring the factor down to $24$ and a uniform design to $1.1$.

\paragraph{The truncated model class.} Fitting a linear or quadratic model to
four cubic systems (design in Methods) produces a fitted field with exact or
approximate symmetries the system does not have (\cref{fig:mechanisms}). An
affine field $Ax + c$ always has a symmetry algebra of dimension at least $n$
in the affine class (exactly $n$ for a generic fit); the threshold rule
declares these in every replication and \symcert{} certifies them in up to
$100\%$. A quadratic fit is less constrained, but its smallest plug-in defect
still falls within the tolerance often enough that the threshold rule declares
a false symmetry in $70\%$ to $100\%$ of replications; \symcert{} certifies one
in a single setting (Van der Pol, $N = 1600$). This is a failure of the
model-class assumption, not of the certificate. In these gross truncations a
lack-of-fit $F$-test against the correct class rejects in $100\%$ of
replications, so gating on it would have prevented every false certification
here; the next subsection shows that this does not generalise.

The same failure arises without anyone choosing to truncate. Sequentially
thresholded least squares, the sparse regression at the core of data-driven
model discovery \citep{brunton2016discovering,kaptanoglu2022pysindy} and the
object of its own convergence theory \citep{zhang2019convergence}, deletes
small coefficients. On the broken-Hopf family $F_\varepsilon =
F_{\mathrm{Hopf}} + \varepsilon x^2\partial_x$ (design in Methods), whose
rotation defect is $0.064$ at $\varepsilon = 0.1$, above the tolerance, a
sparsity threshold of $0.25$ deletes the breaking term, after which the
threshold rule (plug-in defect at most $\delta$) declares the rotation a
symmetry in $100\%$ of replications, against $17\%$ for the same rule on the
unthresholded least-squares fit. Certifying from the unpruned least-squares fit
gives zero.

\subsection{Choosing the model class from the data}
\label{sec:selection}

Here the truth is the Hopf normal form plus $\varepsilon P_k(x)\,\partial_x$,
with $P_k$ the Legendre polynomial of degree $k \in \{5,7\}$. Because $P_k$ is
orthogonal to every polynomial of lower degree on the target box, a degree-$3$
fit sees (up to sampling error) the exactly symmetric Hopf field, while the
true rotation defect grows linearly in $\varepsilon$; the family of degrees
$\{3, k\}$ contains the truth in its largest member. \Cref{fig:selection}
compares five procedures at noise level $0.1$ (Methods).

When the omitted term has degree $5$ the lack-of-fit test is the more sensitive
instrument. At $N = 3200$ a misspecification that it detects with probability
only $0.5$ can produce a true rotation defect of about $0.039$ (to first order
in the omitted part), below the tolerance, and the gate never certified falsely
when the rotation defect differed from the tolerance (\cref{fig:selection}a);
with the rotation defect exactly $\delta$ it certified, in $6\%$ of
replications at $N = 3200$ ($95\%$ interval $3\%$ to $9\%$), a direction whose
true defect lay just above $\delta$. When the omitted term has degree $7$ the
bracket amplifies it more and the test spreads its power over more degrees of
freedom: misspecification invisible at power $0.5$ can produce defects of about
$0.087$, about $1.7$ times the tolerance, and at $N = 3200$ the gate certifies
falsely in $46\%$ of replications ($95\%$ interval $40\%$ to $52\%$) at a
rotation defect of $1.25\delta$, in $27\%$ ($22\%$ to $33\%$) at $1.5\delta$
and in $39\%$ ($33\%$ to $45\%$) at exactly $\delta$, precisely in the range
that \Cref{prop:lof} marks out (\cref{fig:selection}b). The observed rejection
rates agree with the noncentral-$F$ power throughout (largest difference
$0.049$, less than two Monte Carlo standard errors). Choosing the class on one
half of the data and certifying on the other does not remove the problem: it
certifies falsely in up to $11\%$ of replications ($8\%$ to $15\%$) when the
rotation defect exceeds $\delta$ (degree $7$, $N = 12{,}800$, $1.25\delta$) and
in $19\%$ ($15\%$ to $24\%$) when it equals $\delta$ (degree $7$, $N =
12{,}800$), because a class chosen by a test with half the data is more often
too small. The two valid procedures, certifying in the largest class and
certifying simultaneously over both classes, never certified falsely in any of
the $56$ settings. Their price is power (\cref{fig:selection}c): when the truth
is exactly symmetric they certify almost nothing below $N = 51{,}200$ (mean
dimension of the certified subspace at most $0.02$) if the largest class has
degree $5$, and nothing at all in the range studied if it has degree $7$,
whereas the gate certifies the rotation from $N = 3200$. This power is the
price of assuming the larger class, which spares one from trusting a test that
failed to reject; choosing among the nested classes adds only a split of
$\alpha$ (\Cref{prop:classes}).

\subsection{Certified tolerance against the Le~Cam bound}
\label{sec:resolution}

\Cref{fig:headline}c tracks the certified tolerance of the rotation generator
for the exactly symmetric
member ($\varepsilon = 0$) of the broken-Hopf family, whose rotation defect
grows continuously from zero with $\varepsilon$ (the broken members are used
below), against the bound of \Cref{prop:lecam} evaluated on the design of each
replication. Over two decades of $N$ and three noise levels the certified
tolerance follows the $\sigma/\sqrt N$ scaling of the bound (fitted log--log
slopes between $-0.49$ and $-0.52$), exceeding it by a median factor of $8.0$
at each of the noise levels $0.03$, $0.1$ and $0.3$ in the degree-$3$ class
($Q = 20$). In this class the factor is nearly constant in $N$ and $\sigma$,
but it is not constant across model classes: fitting the same symmetric
system in classes of degree $5$ and $7$ ($Q = 42$ and $72$) raises it to
$10.9$ and $13.2$ (medians over $N = 800$ to $12{,}800$, where every bound is
finite and below $1$; \STqsweep). The pointwise bound, valid here because the
rotation is fixed in advance, gives median factors of $8.0$, $10.7$ and $12.9$
over the same range; it is much wider at small $N$ (infinite for degree $7$ at
$N \le 200$) and slightly narrower from $N = 3200$ on (at $N = 12{,}800$,
factors of $7.7$, $10.1$ and $11.6$ against $7.9$, $11.0$ and $13.2$). The
growth of the gap with $Q$ is therefore shared by a bound that is not
simultaneous, so it is not mainly a cost of simultaneity. It reflects the
model class itself, since the bound for one generator depends on every
coefficient that enters its bracket, together with the looseness of the
two-point lower bound, and we cannot apportion it between the two. The
comparison therefore supports the scaling in $N$ and $\sigma$ at a fixed model
class; it does not show that the constant, or the dependence on $Q$, is
optimal.

The same experiment measures the opposite claim. At $N=1600$ and noise level
$0.1$, the lower certificate rules out exact symmetry ($L > 0$) for
$49.5\%$ of replications when the true rotation defect is $0.013$ and for
$100\%$ when it is $0.032$; when the system really is symmetric it does so in
$1.0\%$ of replications (2 of 200; $95\%$ interval $0.1\%$ to $3.6\%$), below
the nominal $5\%$.

\subsection{Problem dimension, and whether to split the sample}
\label{sec:dimension}

The width of the simultaneous certificate is governed by the number of model
coefficients $Q$, through the radius of the confidence ellipsoid; the width of
the split-sample pointwise bound is governed by an effective dimension of the
generator being tested, but it sees half the data. \Cref{fig:power}b,c
settles which wins here: \textbf{the simultaneous certificate is narrower in all
nine configurations}, by factors of $1.6$ to $2.6$, and it certifies the
minimal-defect plug-in direction at least as often in all nine (both averaged
over $N = 200$ to $6400$). Sample splitting, the standard remedy
for the selection effect, is counterproductive here, because the selection
effect it protects against is already handled by simultaneity. Increasing the
\emph{candidate generator} class is nearly free: moving from the
$6$-dimensional affine class to the $12$-dimensional class of all quadratic
generators on the Hopf system changes the certified tolerance (median over
replications, averaged over $N = 200$ to $6400$, as in \cref{fig:power}b) from
$0.0454$ to $0.0447$. Increasing the \emph{model} class is not: raising the
polynomial degree from $3$ to $5$ ($Q$ from $20$ to $42$) widens it from
$0.045$ to $0.122$. Search widely over generators, and spend the
sample-size budget on the model class.

\subsection{Is the guarantee tight?}
\label{sec:tightness}

A certificate that certified nothing would also never certify anything false,
so we measured how conservative ours is (\SFtightness). The coefficient region
covers the true coefficients at close to its nominal level, as
\Cref{prop:closedform} says it should: pooled over the six settings, within
$0.02$ of nominal at every level from $0.50$ to $0.99$ ($0.94$ to $0.96$ in
every setting at nominal $0.95$); single settings, with $80$ replications each,
scatter more widely, from $0.35$ to $0.59$ at nominal $0.50$, within Monte
Carlo error over the $36$ setting-level combinations. The certificate interval
contains the true defect of every plug-in direction in all replications at
every nominal level from $0.50$ to $0.99$. Each bound is a worst case over a
region for all $Q$ coefficients, and the pointwise bound for a generator fixed
in advance is nearly as wide (\Cref{sec:resolution}), so this conservatism is
not mainly a cost of simultaneity over generators.

To see what a finite-sample guarantee costs, we compared the certificate with a
calibration-\emph{targeting} alternative, the parametric bootstrap that draws
$\beta^* \sim N(\bhat,\hat\Sigma)$ and reports the $\alpha$ and $1-\alpha$
quantiles of $\defect_\nu(\xi_\theta;F_{\beta^*})$ \citep{efron1979bootstrap},
on the rotation generator of the broken-Hopf family. Its upper bound is $5\%$
to $42\%$ lower than the certificate's at $\alpha = 0.05$ and up to $47\%$
lower at $\alpha = 0.2$, the gap being largest at a true symmetry and shrinking
with $N$ when the defect is not zero. For this generator, fixed in advance, the
bootstrap upper bound held: with the true defect just above the tolerance
($1.02\delta$) and sample sizes from $800$ to $51{,}200$, it certified falsely
in at most $15\%$ of replications at nominal $\alpha = 0.5$ ($95\%$ interval
$11\%$ to $20\%$), $4.3\%$ at $0.2$ ($2.3\%$ to $7.3\%$) and $0.7\%$ at $0.05$
($0.1\%$ to $2.4\%$), all within its nominal levels, while \symcert{} never
did. Near a symmetry, where the defect, a norm, is not a smooth function of the
coefficients and its plug-in value is biased upward, the bootstrap fails in its
lower quantile, which lies above the true defect: its interval covered the
truth in at most one of $200$ replications at either level ($\alpha = 0.05$ and
$0.2$, nominal $90\%$ and $60\%$) whenever the true defect was $0.01$ or less,
and at $0.02$ ($N = 3200$) in $36\%$ ($29\%$ to $43\%$) against a nominal
$90\%$ and $8.5\%$ ($5.0\%$ to $13.3\%$) against $60\%$, so it would wrongly
exclude exact symmetry.
The certificate's coverage never fell below $0.93$ (nominal $0.80$) or $0.985$
(nominal $0.95$). The bootstrap upper bound itself fails for a generator
selected from the data (\SFtightness e). On four systems without symmetry (Van
der Pol, Duffing, Lotka--Volterra and Sel'kov, all on the box $[-1,1]^2$,
smallest true defects $0.25$ to $0.64$; $N = 50$ to $400$, noise levels $0.2$
and $0.5$, $300$ replications per setting), with the generator of smallest
plug-in defect bounded as if it had been fixed in advance, its coverage was
below nominal in $9$ of $32$ settings at nominal $0.80$ and in $8$ of $32$ at
$0.95$, with the whole $95\%$ interval below nominal in $5$ settings at each
level, nine of these ten on the Duffing oscillator (worst $0.59$, $95\%$
interval $0.54$ to $0.65$, and $0.90$, $0.86$ to $0.93$, at $N = 50$ and noise
level $0.5$): selection biases the plug-in defect downward, by up to $0.15$ on
average, and a bootstrap centred at it does not see the selection. The
certificate's upper bound contained the true defect in every replication. With
true defects far above $\delta$, the failure shows as an understated tolerance,
not as a false certification at $0.05$. The extra width is therefore the price
of a guarantee that holds by theorem, in finite samples, simultaneously over
generators and for both bounds, and the experiment with a data-selected
generator, on the Duffing oscillator in particular, shows a case in which a
bound without this guarantee fails.

\subsection{Departures from the assumptions}
\label{sec:robustness}

\input{\tabdir robustness}

\Cref{tab:robustness} separates the two coverages that matter: that of the
coefficient region, the event on which every certificate rests, and that of the
certificate itself. With independent rows, the exact least-squares region keeps
close to its nominal level under Student-$t_4$ and Laplace errors (at least
$0.938$) but not under heteroskedastic errors ($0.82$), and White's sandwich
undercovers at the smaller sample size ($0.77$ to $0.88$ at $N = 400$, a
known small-sample weakness of the plain sandwich with a chi-square reference)
and approaches its nominal level at $N = 3200$ ($0.93$ to $0.96$). The
certificate covered the true defect of every plug-in direction in every one of
these replications and never certified falsely. Its own conservatism absorbed
the region's shortfall here, but that margin is not part of any guarantee, and
the region is what should be chosen to match the error structure.

On trajectory data the rows are dependent through the design, but with exact
states and independent errors the regression is still a correctly specified
least-squares problem, and White's region covers ($0.90$ to $0.98$). The
cluster-robust region, which also allows dependent errors within a trajectory,
covers less well with $48$ trajectories ($0.76$ to $0.91$): its covariance
estimate has about as many degrees of freedom as there are trajectories, here
only $1.6$ to $2.4$ times the number of coefficients ($Q = 30$ or $20$), even
in the jackknife form that corrects the leverage bias of the plain cluster
sandwich. With noisy states and central differences inside each trajectory,
state noise of $0.05\%$ to $0.5\%$ of the box half-width barely biases the
fit; differencing multiplies it into large derivative noise, which costs
detection, and makes neighbouring errors negatively correlated, which the
cluster-robust region accounts for and White's overestimates.

Genuinely dependent errors are another matter (\SFdependence{} and
\STdependence). With errors
following an AR(1) process along each trajectory, the OLS and White regions,
which assume independent errors, contained the true coefficients in at most
$7\%$ of replications once the autocorrelation $\phi$ reached $0.5$, and for
$\phi \ge 0.9$ the certificate built on them failed as well, its interval
covering the true defects in only $1\%$ to $27\%$ of replications; in these
two systems the failure happened not to produce a false certification, but
nothing guarantees that. The cluster-robust region, designed for this case,
covered in $73\%$ to $92\%$ of replications with $48$ trajectories and $91\%$
to $95\%$ with $192$, and the certificate built on it covered in every
replication; with $12$ trajectories, fewer than the coefficients, it leaves
directions unidentified and certifies nothing. The Newey--West region, whose
justification is asymptotic in the length of each series, was not adequate
for trajectories of at most $100$ points: for $\phi \ge 0.5$ its coverage was
$0.14$ to $0.80$ with $192$ trajectories, $0.01$ to $0.81$ with $48$ and $0$
to $0.46$ with $12$, and it fell short already at $\phi = 0.5$, where the
correlation time is only a few steps ($0.75$ to $0.80$ with $192$). The
certificate built on it covered in at least $98\%$ of replications in every
other setting, but with $12$ trajectories at $\phi = 0.98$ in only $80\%$
(Hopf) and $91\%$ (rigid body), though without a false certification. Dependent
data therefore need both the right estimator and enough independent
trajectories.

Noise in the states is not covered, and it can manufacture symmetry
(\SFeiv). We observed the states with independent noise of standard deviation
$\sigma_x$ and the right-hand side separately with small independent noise.
Least squares then estimates a pseudo-true field whose nonlinear terms are
attenuated. For the hardening Duffing oscillator $\dot x = y$,
$\dot y = -x - 0.3x^3 - 0.2y$, whose smallest true defect is $0.102$, state
noise of $\sigma_x = 0.08$ lowers the smallest defect of the pseudo-true field
to $0.038$. As data accumulate the region concentrates on that field, not on
the truth (it contained the true coefficients in none of the replications), and
\symcert{} certified a false symmetry in every replication at
$N = 25{,}600$ and $204{,}800$; at $N = 3200$ it was still too wide to certify.
The threshold rule did so at every sample size. State noise of $0.04$ or $0.12$
left the pseudo-true defect above the tolerance and produced no false
certification, and on the symmetric Hopf system state noise of $0.08$ or more
destroyed detection instead. Whether errors in the states inflate or deflate the
apparent symmetry depends on the system, which is why the guarantee cannot
extend to them without a model of those errors.

\SFmodelerror{} turns to systems that no polynomial class contains (one data
set of $N = 40{,}000$ points per system and degree, noise level $0.02$). On a
non-polynomial planar flow with an exact rotational symmetry, on the box
$[-1,1]^2$, a degree-$5$ fit gives an upper bound of $0.050$ if model error is
ignored, a bound with no guarantee. Paying for the error in $L^2$ against a
degree-$9$ class, with its size taken from the known truth (an oracle,
unavailable in practice), gives $0.65$, and paying in supremum norm $2.65$.
Bounding the omitted part from the data instead gives $0.68$, close to the
oracle $L^2$ value. Both $L^2$ values assume that the omitted part lies in the
degree-$9$ class, which for this flow holds only approximately, so they are
sensitivity values rather than guaranteed bounds; only the supremum-norm
value is a guaranteed bound, provided its oracle sizes bound the true suprema
(here they are maxima over $4\times10^{5}$ points, which can fall slightly
short of them). All three are vacuous at
$\delta = 0.05$. Raising the fitted degree to $9$ shrinks the relative $L^2$
model error to $0.5\%$ and the data-driven bound to $0.27$, still too wide to
be useful: the bracket differentiates, so the constant converting a bound on
$\|h\|$ into a bound on $\|[\xi,h]\|$ grows with the degree of the class faster
than the error falls. On the damped pendulum, which has no continuous symmetry,
the same accounting is cheap (true defect $0.204$, bounds between $0.23$ and
$0.39$ across degrees and forms, with the same caveat for the $L^2$ forms)
because a degree-$5$ polynomial reproduces $\sin$ on the target domain to a
relative $L^2$ error of $0.07\%$.

When a dictionary that contains the truth is available, no model-error term is
needed (\STdictionary). We fitted the same planar flow, with the unit disc as
target domain, with quadratic polynomials and four radial functions, with
and without the symmetry-breaking term $\varepsilon x^2\partial_x$, and the
damped pendulum with a polynomial and trigonometric library. Across $45$
settings ($300$ replications each) \symcert{} never certified falsely, while a
threshold at the tolerance did so in up to $6.3\%$ of replications. With the
exact rotational symmetry present, it was certified in every replication at
noise level $0.03$, with a median upper bound of $0.008$ at $N = 3200$; the
polynomial fits above, on the box $[-1,1]^2$ with $N = 40{,}000$ and noise
level $0.02$, gave only vacuous bounds once model error was paid for. With a
breaking term giving the rotation a defect of $0.024$ \symcert{} certified
that approximate symmetry from $N = 800$ at the same noise level, and with a
defect of $0.096$ it certified nothing. On the pendulum the lower certificate
excluded every $0.05$-approximate symmetry in all replications at $N = 3200$
for noise levels up to $0.1$, and the upper bound on the minimal defect
($0.213$ at noise level $0.03$) was within $5\%$ of its true value ($0.204$).

\subsection{Scaling, units and closure}
\label{sec:scaling}

The state dimension enters twice: through the number of model coefficients $Q$,
which sets the radius of the confidence region, and through the number of
candidate generators $P$, which sets how many directions are searched. On
linear systems in $n = 2,\dots,6$ built from rotation blocks, whose symmetry
algebra is the commutant of the coefficient matrix and has dimension $n$, false
certification is zero in all $20$ cells (\STscaling; five dimensions, $N = 200$, $800$,
$3200$ and $12{,}800$) while the search space $P = n^2$ grows from $4$ to $36$.
What the growth costs is width: at $N = 12{,}800$ the median upper bound of the
minimal-defect plug-in direction rises from $0.0017$ at $n=2$ ($Q=6$) to
$0.0050$ at $n=6$ ($Q=42$), a factor $2.9$, somewhat more than the $\sqrt{Q}$
factor of $2.6$. Because $P$ and $Q$ grow together in this design, it does not
show which of the two the width depends on; at a fixed model class, enlarging
the candidate class barely changes the width (\Cref{sec:dimension}). The
dimension of the certified subspace reaches $n$ (detection) in every
replication from $N = 800$ at $n = 4$ and from $N = 3200$ at $n = 6$, and one
certified plug-in subspace costs between $0.03$ and $0.11$ CPU-seconds across
this range.

Units matter exactly as \Cref{lem:props} predicts (\STunits). Re-expressing one
coordinate of the broken-Hopf system in units $c$ times larger, for $c$ from
$0.01$ to $100$, moves the Euclidean defect of the rotation between $0.051$ and
$0.076$ (it is $0.064$ in the original units), and lowers the smallest
Euclidean defect in the class for Lotka--Volterra from $0.27$ to $0.0035$; with
the nondimensional metric neither number moves. Certifying in the rescaled
Lotka--Volterra coordinates with the Euclidean metric at $N = 12{,}800$
certified a generator in every replication, and every one of those
certifications was false when judged by the unit-free defect of the system;
with the declared metric nothing was certified.

Certified subspaces of dimension two or more were close to closed under the
bracket (\STclosure). For the radial sphere flow, whose symmetry algebra is the
non-abelian $\mathfrak{so}(3)$, the certified subspace had dimension three in
every replication except at the smallest sample size with the larger noise,
the part of each commutator outside it was at most $0.4\%$ of the size of its
two terms, and every commutator was itself certified. For the two
abelian algebras the commutators were negligible.

\subsection{A physically expected symmetry in ocean measurements}
\label{sec:drifters}

Near-inertial currents in the upper ocean follow, to first order, the damped
slab model $\dot u = f v - r u$, $\dot v = -f u - r v$ plus wind forcing
\citep{pollard1970comparison}, where $(u,v)$ is the horizontal velocity and $f$
the local Coriolis parameter. With time measured in units of $1/f$ the
right-hand side is $(v - (r/f)\,u,\; -u - (r/f)\,v)$, a uniform damping plus a
quarter-turn rotation of the velocity, both of which commute with the rotation
generator: the dynamics are rotation invariant. We asked whether hourly
velocities of drogued drifters of the Global Drifter Program
\citep{elipot2016global,elipot2022hourly} certify that symmetry, using
$46{,}733$ velocity--acceleration pairs from $31$ drifters in the North Pacific
(30--45$^\circ$N, 180--140$^\circ$W, June to September 2016--2019) after
isolating the near-inertial band (Methods, \STdrifters). The fitted linear
field is $\dot u = 0.994\,v$, $\dot v = -1.028\,u$ in units of $f$, the
textbook inertial oscillation, and the plug-in defect of the rotation generator
is $0.025$. Because rows along a trajectory are serially dependent, we use the
cluster-robust region over drifters, which needs no bandwidth: the rotation is
then certified as a $0.074$-approximate symmetry of the linear model ($0.067$
to $0.074$ across filter windows, so not at $\delta = 0.05$), and exact
rotation invariance is not excluded ($L = 0$). The OLS region, which ignores
the dependence, and the Newey--West region, which allows serial correlation
within each drifter's record, would both certify at $\delta = 0.05$ and exclude
exact invariance (upper bounds $0.040$ and $0.045$, lower bounds $0.010$ and
$0.005$). We report the Newey--West result as a sensitivity result only,
because it depends on an estimated bandwidth and on asymptotics in the length
of each record, whereas the cluster-robust region assumes nothing about the
form or range of the dependence within a drifter. The dependence study above,
in which Newey--West undercovered, used series of at most $100$ points, much
shorter than most drifter records here (median $1{,}486$ pairs), so it is only
indirect evidence. The verdict at $\delta = 0.05$ depends on this choice.
Quadratic or cubic terms widen the cluster-robust bound to $0.11$ and $0.21$,
and the construction of \Cref{prop:classes} applied over degrees $1$ to $3$
with cluster-robust regions gives $0.25$ (the proposition is proved for the
exact regions; with these regions the bound holds only asymptotically). The
result rests on assumptions the data cannot check: a linear model class;
velocities without measurement error (their median standard error is
$0.014$~m\,s$^{-1}$ against a root-mean-square of $0.109$~m\,s$^{-1}$); errors
uncorrelated with the velocity, although they contain the unmodelled wind
forcing, which also drives the velocity (rotation-invariant forcing statistics
would bias the fit without creating a defect; anisotropic forcing could create
or hide one); independence between drifters, which share wind forcing within a
region and season; and an asymptotic region with $31$ clusters, five times the
$6$ coefficients of the linear class but only $2.6$ and $1.6$ times those of
the quadratic and cubic classes. We report it as a case study within those
assumptions.

                                                    Abstract
          Continuous symmetries of dynamical systems are often discovered from data as the nullspace
      of a linear operator. With noisy data, a symmetry is declared whenever a singular value looks
      small. That practice has no error control, and it points the wrong way: failing to reject a
      symmetry is not evidence for it. We recast symmetry discovery as an equivalence test: a
      candidate generator is certified as an approximate symmetry only when the data rule out a
      violation larger than a stated tolerance. For vector-field models that are linear in their coefficients,
      such as polynomial or dictionary models, a scale-free measure of symmetry violation is a ratio of
      norms of linear images of the coefficients, so a single confidence region yields bounds valid for
      every candidate generator at once. On ten systems with exactly known symmetries and 72,000
      simulated data sets, the procedure never certified a false symmetry, whereas a fixed threshold
      did so in 0.5% of runs on average and in up to 63% in its worst setting. The guarantee requires
      a model class containing the true system and exactly observed states; we show that violating
      either can manufacture false symmetries, and how to detect or avoid this.


1    Introduction
Symmetry is one of the few inductive biases that reliably pays for itself in machine learning, from
group-equivariant convolutions [22] to general constructions for arbitrary matrix groups [32] and
the geometric programme that organises them [14]. All of it presumes that the symmetry is known.
When it is not, it has to be discovered from data, and the question this paper is about is what a
discovery may honestly claim.
     A continuous symmetry of a dynamical system ẋ = F (x) is a one-parameter family of state
transformations that leaves the dynamics unchanged. Each such family is generated by a vector
field ξ, and ξ generates a symmetry exactly when the Lie bracket [ξ, F ] vanishes identically. Because
the bracket is linear in ξ, the set of generators is the nullspace of a linear operator. This observation
drives a large body of work: it is how symmetries of point clouds [16], of trained networks [34, 48]
and of governing equations [38] are found, and it was recently unified and substantially generalised
by Otto et al. [51], who show that enforcing, discovering and promoting symmetry are all governed
by the same Lie-derivative operator, with discovery reduced to computing a nullspace.
     With real data the operator must be estimated, and an estimated operator has no exact nullspace.
Practice therefore replaces “is this singular value zero?” with “is this singular value small?”, by
comparing it to a tolerance or by looking for a gap in the spectrum. This fails in two ways.


                                                         1
    The tolerance means nothing in particular. A singular value of the Lie-derivative operator
has the units of the data and depends on the arbitrary scaling of the basis chosen for the candidate
generators; a tolerance of 10−3 is a statement about neither the system nor the evidence. Whether
a small value indicates a symmetry or merely a fortunate draw of the noise depends on the noise
level, the sample size, the conditioning of the design and the dimension of the model class, none of
which the tolerance sees.
    The burden of proof is inverted. Suppose we improve matters by attaching a hypothesis
test, as a rank test would [40, 54]: test H0 : “this generator is an exact symmetry” and declare a
symmetry when the test does not reject. Such a test is valid, rejecting a true symmetry at most 5%
of the time, and it is still the wrong instrument, because failing to reject H0 is not evidence for
H0 . The error we care about, declaring a symmetry that does not exist, is the type-II error, and no
level-α test controls it. In our experiments this is not a technicality: a valid level-0.05 rank test
declares a symmetry that is not one in 7% of replications on average over our settings and in all of
them in the worst setting.

Our reframing. A procedure that may honestly say “ξ is a symmetry” must be able to rule
out the alternative. So we ask for an equivalence test [8, 57, 60]: fix a tolerance δ, and certify ξ
only when the data reject the hypothesis that its defect exceeds δ. This buys error control in the
direction that matters: the probability of certifying a generator whose true defect exceeds δ is at
most α. It also forces the tolerance into the open, where it can be argued about.
    Two ingredients make it practical. First, we measure symmetry violation with a relative defect:
the norm of the bracket [ξ, F ] divided by the norm√ of the two terms whose cancellation produces it
(Definition 1). The resulting number lies in [0, 2], does not change when ξ or F is rescaled or the
candidate class is reparameterised, and answers a concrete question: by what fraction do the two
halves of the bracket fail to cancel? Once the region of interest and the scales of the state variables
are declared, a tolerance in these units means the same thing for every system. Second, for a model
that is linear in its coefficients β (a polynomial vector field, or any fixed dictionary of functions), the
squared defect is a ratio of squared norms of linear images of β (Lemma 2). A confidence ellipsoid
for β therefore propagates through it by optimising over an ellipsoid, which reduces to trust-region
subproblems [33, 47] that can be solved globally and with certified accuracy. Because a single event
(β lies in its confidence region) implies the bound for every generator at once, the certificate is
simultaneous over the whole candidate space, with no correction for selection or multiplicity and no
sample splitting. Each of these steps is elementary on its own; the contribution is the formulation
that makes them fit together.

What we find. We evaluate on ten polynomial dynamical systems whose symmetry algebras
we compute exactly by rational linear algebra, so that every error is scored against ground truth
rather than against a tolerance. Across 240 noise and sample-size settings (72,000 replications), our
procedure, SymCert, never once certifies a generator whose true defect exceeds the tolerance (the
one-sided 95% upper bound on its error rate is 0.010 in every setting). A fixed threshold at the
same tolerance errs in 0.5% of replications on average and in 63% in its worst setting; an eigengap
heuristic and the rank and split-sample significance tests reach 100%, 100% and 92% in their worst
settings. This error control costs detection power, and we report the cost. Two non-polynomial
systems fitted with dictionaries that contain them behave in the same way.
    Four further findings, each a consequence of taking the statistics seriously.
    Additive derivative noise does not manufacture symmetry; it hides it. When the right-hand side
is measured with noise and the states exactly, the plug-in defect is biased upward, so noise costs

detections, not false symmetries.
    Coverage has a computable price. If the data occupy a region µ while the claim is about a
domain ν, the defect on ν can exceed the defect measured on µ by at most an explicit factor built
from two generalised eigenvalues of Gram matrices (Proposition 6); the factor is infinite exactly
when the data lie in the zero set of a possible bracket, as on a single trajectory that settles onto
an attractor. Across 50 coverage settings, spurious symmetry appeared only where that factor
permitted it, and never in the 13 settings where it proves the contrary.
    The model class and noisy states do manufacture symmetry. Fitting a class too small to contain
the truth produces a model with symmetries the system does not have; sparse regression, the
standard tool of data-driven model discovery [15], does this automatically on a weakly symmetry-
breaking system by deleting the term that breaks the symmetry; and noise in the states attenuates
nonlinear terms until the fitted model looks more linear, and therefore more symmetric, than the
system is. Our certificate does not repair these failures on its own, because its validity is conditional
on a correctly specified class and exactly observed states. A lack-of-fit test is not a remedy either:
we show exactly how large a symmetry violation a test that fails to reject can hide (Proposition 3),
and give a procedure that remains valid when the class is chosen from the data, provided the largest
class considered contains the truth (Proposition 4).
    Units matter unless the metric is declared. With a Euclidean norm on the state, changing the
units of one coordinate can turn a clear absence of symmetry into an apparent one; declaring the
scales of the state variables removes the dependence (Lemma 1).

2.1    Symmetry discovery as a nullspace problem
Generators and the Lie derivative. Let ẋ = F (x) be a dynamical system on Rn and let ξ be a
vector field on Rn . The flow of ξ is a one-parameter group of state transformations, and it maps
solutions of ẋ = F (x) to solutions if and only if the Lie bracket

                                   Lξ F = [ξ, F ] = DF ξ − Dξ F                                       (1)

vanishes identically [50, Ch. 2]; here DF and Dξ are Jacobian matrices. Equation (1) is the Lie
derivative in the sense of Otto et al. [51, Eq. 16]; the same expression, with Dξ F replaced by the
appropriate output action, governs symmetries of a learned map, and with Dξ F deleted it governs
invariances of a learned scalar function (Supplementary Note 1).                                P
    We search inside a candidate class V = span{ζ1 , . . . , ζP } of vector fields, writing ξθ = p θp ζp ;
in our experiments V is the set of affine generators ξ(x) = Sx + b, whose flows form the affine
group, or all polynomial fields of degree at most two. Because equation (1) is linear in ξ, the exact
symmetry algebra inside V is the nullspace {θ : [ξθ , F ] ≡ 0}, andR it is standard to obtain it as the
nullspace of the positive semi-definite Gram operator ⟨η, Sξ⟩ = (Lη F )⊤ (Lξ F ) dµ [51, §7].

state space and about how vector fields are measured there. We therefore fix a target measure ν,

Lemma 1 (Properties). Where it is defined, ρν ∈ [0, 2], and ρν (ξ; F ) = 0 if and only if ξ generates
a symmetry of F on the support of ν. It is invariant under ξ 7→ c ξ and F 7→ c′ F for nonzero c, c′ ,
hence under any invertible reparameterisation of the candidate class. Under an invertible affine
change of coordinates x = Az + b, with ν transported to the new coordinates and W replaced by
A⊤ W A, it is unchanged. With the metric held fixed at W = I it is unchanged only under similarity
transformations (A = cQ, Q orthogonal, c > 0).

   The proof is in Methods. ρ is the fraction of the bracket’s two terms that fails to cancel, so
ρ = 0.05 means the two halves of the bracket agree to 5% in root-mean-square over the target
domain. A generator with ρν (ξ; F ) ≤ δ is what we call a δ-approximate symmetry; δ plays the role

that the singular-value tolerance played before, but now it does not depend on the scale of F , on
the scale of ξ, or on how the candidate basis is chosen.
    It does depend on the units of the state when W is held fixed. Measuring one coordinate of
a Lotka–Volterra system in units a hundred times smaller lowers the smallest Euclidean defect in
the affine class from 0.27 to 0.0035 (Supplementary Table S6), which would turn a clear absence
of symmetry into an apparent one. The remedy is to declare the metric: either nondimensionalise
each coordinate by a characteristic scale si of the problem, or, equivalently, use W = diag(1/s2i )
with si expressed in the current units. Either way the defect, and hence the meaning of a tolerance,
becomes independent of the units.
    One class of generators is outside the reach of the defect. A translation ξ = b has Dξ = 0,
so the second term of the bracket vanishes and ρν (b; F ) is 1 whenever DF b ̸= 0 and undefined
when it is zero: the ratio says nothing about how close a translation is to a symmetry. Certifying
translation symmetries needs a different normalisation of ∥DF b∥; the machinery below applies to
any normalisation that is the norm of a linear image of the model coefficients, but we do not pursue
this here.

Remark 1 (Small eigenvalues are hard to read). Even with a scale fixed, the smallest eigenvalue of
an estimated Gram matrix is a delicate object: its fluctuations are of a different order from those of
the bulk, and the classical asymptotics [2] and their random-matrix refinements [37] show that the
map from “small” to “zero in the population” is not one a fixed threshold can implement. This is
why the certificate below is stated for the defect ratio directly rather than for eigenvalues.

Remark 2 (An average, not a supremum). ρν is a mean-square quantity, so a generator can have a
small defect while violating the symmetry badly on a small part of the domain, a property it shares
with the Gram-operator formulation it refines and with every L2 -based criterion. Two responses
are available, each with a cost: choose ν to weight the region whose behaviour matters, which is
the intended use of making ν explicit; or replace the numerator by a supremum norm, which for
polynomials is bounded by a constant times the L2 norm through a Markov-type inequality, at the
cost of that constant.

    Without a normalisation the “small singular value” criterion is not even well posed: rescaling
one basis generator ζp by 103 rescales the corresponding row and column of the Gram matrix by
103 and 106 , moving eigenvalues across any fixed threshold. ρ is a functional of the vector field ξ
itself and is immune to this.

true system: an upper bound on ρν (ξθ ; Fβ ⋆ ) that holds with a stated probability, for whichever
generator the analysis happens to select.

    (ii) A class selected from the data. A natural workflow enlarges the class until a lack-of-fit F -test

(the test may simply lack power; this is the same inversion of the burden of proof that motivates the
equivalence formulation), and the region in a data-selected class is not a post-selection confidence
region. The first reason can be quantified.

The errors. Exact coverage of the least-squares region needs Gaussian homoskedastic errors

asymptotically in the number of rows; the cluster-robust sandwich for independent trajectories with
arbitrary dependence within each, asymptotically in the number of trajectories, which must exceed
the number of coefficients for the region to be bounded in every direction; the Newey–West sandwich
[49] with an automatic bandwidth [3] for serially correlated errors along long trajectories. Noisy
states are a different matter: the regressors then carry error, least squares estimates a pseudo-true
field, and no covariance estimate repairs the bias. That case is not covered (Section 2.12).

Additive derivative noise does not manufacture symmetry. It is natural to expect that
noise makes small singular values smaller. Under model (3), that is, with noise on a measured

pulls the other way: the generator chosen to minimise the plug-in defect is selected for a small
value (the winner’s curse). Both effects are measured in Section 2.7, and Proposition 1 removes the
need to reason about the second. The statement is specific to its setting: noise in the states acts
quite differently, by attenuating nonlinear terms, and does manufacture symmetry (Section 2.12).

2.5   Experimental design
Ten polynomial systems on R2 and R3 serve as the main benchmarks, four with a nonzero symmetry
algebra inside the affine candidate class and six with none (Methods). Their symmetry algebras

ground truth involves no tolerance and no sampling, which matters because the quantity
being estimated is itself a near-degeneracy. Every method is scored identically, by the rate of false

subspace containing the true algebra to within 10◦ ). Rates are reported with exact binomial intervals

exact least-squares ellipsoid.

2.10                Problem dimension, and whether to split the sample
The width of the simultaneous certificate is governed by the number of model coefficients Q, through
the radius of the confidence ellipsoid; the width of the split-sample pointwise bound is governed
by an effective dimension of the generator being tested, but it sees half the data. Figure 5(b,c)
settles which wins here: the simultaneous certificate is narrower in all nine configurations,
by factors of 1.6 to 2.6, and its detection rate is at least as high in all nine. Sample splitting, the
standard remedy for the selection effect, is counterproductive here, because the selection effect it
protects against is already handled by simultaneity. Increasing the candidate generator class is
nearly free: moving from the 6-dimensional affine class to the 12-dimensional class of all quadratic
generators on the Hopf system changes the mean certified tolerance from 0.0454 to 0.0447. Increasing
the model class is not: raising the polynomial degree from 3 to 5 (Q from 20 to 42) widens it from
0.045 to 0.122. Search widely over generators, and spend the sample-size budget on the model class.

2.14                         A physically expected symmetry in ocean measurements
Near-inertial currents in the upper ocean follow, to first order, the damped slab model u̇ = f v − ru,
v̇ = −f u − rv plus wind forcing [52], where (u, v) is the horizontal velocity and f the local Coriolis
parameter. In units of 1/f the right-hand side is (−rI + J)(u, v)⊤ with J the planar rotation, which
commutes with the rotation generator: the dynamics are rotation invariant. We asked whether
hourly velocities of drogued drifters of the Global Drifter Program [29, 30] certify that symmetry,
using 46,733 velocity–acceleration pairs from 31 drifters in the North Pacific (30–45◦ N, 180–140◦ W,
June to September 2016–2019) after isolating the near-inertial band (Methods, Supplementary
Table S8). The fitted linear field is u̇ = 0.994 v, v̇ = −1.028 u in units of f , the textbook inertial
oscillation, and the plug-in defect of the rotation generator is 0.025. Because rows along a trajectory
are serially dependent, the appropriate region is the cluster-robust one over drifters; with it the
rotation is certified as a 0.072-approximate symmetry of the linear model (0.066 to 0.072 across
filter windows), and exact rotation invariance is not excluded (L = 0). A region that ignores the
dependence would report a bound of 0.040 and a lower bound of 0.010, that is, it would certify at
δ = 0.05 and at the same time claim the symmetry is not exact; neither claim is supported. Allowing
quadratic or cubic terms widens the bound to 0.10 and 0.19. The result rests on two assumptions
the data cannot check, a linear model class and velocities without measurement error (their median
standard error is 0.014 m s−1 against a root-mean-square of 0.109 m s−1 ), and we report it as a case
study within those assumptions.

3     Discussion
In practice the procedure changes what is reported. Fix the region ν where the symmetry is supposed
to hold, the scales of the state variables, the candidate class and a tolerance δ on the relative defect.
Fit a model class that is assumed to contain the truth, or certify simultaneously over a nested family
whose largest member is (Proposition 4). Compute U (θ), certify what falls below δ, and report U
itself rather than a binary verdict, because it is the smallest tolerance the data support and the
honest analogue of the singular value that would otherwise be reported; report κ so the reader can
see how much of the target domain the data actually visited. The reporting checklist in Methods
lists every choice that gives the tolerance its meaning.
     The limitations follow from Table 1. The model class is the binding assumption: validity
requires the truth to lie in the fitted class, in the largest class of a nested family, or within an
assumed distance of it. A fitted model can be more symmetric than the system it models, and no
analysis of that model alone can detect the difference. A lack-of-fit test that fails to reject does
not establish correct specification, and Proposition 3 shows that the misspecification it misses can
produce true defects larger than the width of the certificate. Noisy states are not covered: least

                                                        18
squares then targets a pseudo-true field, and in our experiments the certificate concentrates on
a spurious symmetry as data accumulate; integral or weak formulations of model discovery [46],
ensemble resampling [31] and explicit errors-in-variables methods [18] are the natural routes, and
none is part of the guarantee here. The certificate is conservative: its interval contains the truth
in essentially every replication even at nominal level 0.5, mainly because the confidence region is
simultaneous over all Q coefficient directions, and at a fixed model class it is a constant factor above
the Le Cam lower bound, a gap that grows with Q; part of that gap is the looseness of a two-point
bound, and we cannot apportion it. We treat connected symmetry groups whose generators lie in a
prescribed finite-dimensional class, excluding translations, for which the relative defect degenerates,
and moderate problem sizes (n ≤ 6, Q ≤ 110, P ≤ 36). Discrete symmetries, and symmetries
visible only after a nonlinear change of coordinates [44, 63], are outside the framework, as they
are outside the Lie-algebraic setting generally. Finally, the benchmarks are simulated deliberately,
because measuring an error rate requires knowing the truth exactly, which a real system does not
provide. Of the two real data sets we analyse, the lynx–hare records are too small to support any
symmetry claim (Supplementary Note 2), and the drifter velocities certify a physically expected
rotation invariance to a stated tolerance, but only under a model class and an observation model
that the data cannot check; neither replaces a setting in which symmetries are both real and known.
    The work sits among several lines of research. The idea that continuous symmetries are a
nullspace runs through work on point clouds [16], conserved quantities [38], trained networks [34, 48]
and dynamic mode decomposition [5]; Otto et al. [51] unify these, extend them to nonlinear group
actions on manifolds and to sections of vector bundles, and add convex penalties that promote
symmetry during training. Their conclusion names our problem: it will be important “to study the
perturbative effects of noisy data in algorithms to discover and promote symmetry, with the goal
of understanding the effects of problem dimension, noise level, and amount of data.” We answer
with confidence statements rather than with a perturbation analysis, because a perturbation bound
alone does not tell a user what to declare; the Weyl-inequality baseline in our experiments is the
perturbation answer, and it is valid but never certifies. They also ask whether their framework
can “find elements that are nearly symmetric, and to bound the degree of asymmetry”; Definition 1
is such a bound and SymCert supplies its confidence statement, in a setting where approximate
symmetry is widely recognised in modelling [55, 59]. An alternative line searches for generators
by nonlinear optimisation or adversarial training [26, 27, 44, 62, 63], and a related line learns
invariances as hyperparameters through augmentation or marginal likelihood [7, 36]. These methods
return a point estimate of a generator with no error control, and the question asked here, when a
discovered generator may be declared and at what tolerance, applies to them too: whenever the
Lie derivative is bilinear in the generator coordinates and the model coefficients the machinery
transfers, and for a fully nonlinear model the ellipsoid must be replaced by a confidence region
obtained some other way, with the logic of Proposition 1 unchanged. Once a symmetry is in
hand it is enforced as hard constraints or convex penalties [51], as physics-informed losses [1, 53],
as data augmentation [13], or inside equation discovery itself [64], and everything downstream
inherits the reliability of the discovery step. On the statistical side, equivalence testing is standard
wherever the interesting hypothesis is the null [8, 57, 60]; our contribution is not the idea but the
observation that symmetry discovery is one of those settings, with a computable construction for
this functional. Rank tests [24, 40, 54] attack the same nullspace-dimension question and place
symmetry in the null. The selection of a generator is the issue addressed by post-selection and
split-sample inference [4, 9, 19, 41, 45]; here simultaneity over the whole candidate class dissolves
it more cheaply than splitting, which we verify empirically, whereas selection of the model class
needs the separate treatment of Proposition 4, in line with the general difficulty of inference after
model selection [43]. Because a single event covers all generators at once, no multiplicity correction

                                                  19
of the Benjamini–Hochberg kind [6] is needed. Finally, the shape of the problem (many moment
conditions, a low-dimensional parameter, and a test of whether they can all be satisfied) is that of a
test of overidentifying restrictions [35], with the difference that here the restriction of interest is the
one we would like to accept rather than reject.
    Symmetry discovery is an instance of a pattern that recurs whenever a scientific claim takes
the form “this quantity is zero”: a rank, a conservation law, a conditional independence, a null
effect. In each, an estimate is compared to a tolerance, and the tolerance is asked to carry statistical
weight it cannot bear. The remedy is the same each time: state the tolerance in interpretable units,
and require the data to exclude the alternative instead of merely failing to detect it. Whether that
remedy is cheap depends on the functional; for the Lie derivative of a model that is linear in its
coefficients it costs a sequence of trust-region subproblems, and its reach is set by how honestly the
model class and the observation model can be specified.

4.4   Model-class procedures
The lack-of-fit test is the F -test of the smaller class against the larger on the same rows, with
residual sums of squares from both fits, n(mH − m) and n(N − mH ) degrees of freedom, and level
0.05; it does not share its variance estimate with the certificate, which uses the residual variance
of the class in which it is computed. The gate chooses the smallest class whose test against the
largest does not reject and certifies there; no error budget is allocated to the test, because the gate
carries no guarantee. Split applies the gate to the first half of the rows and certifies on the second.
Largest certifies in the largest class at level α. Simultaneous implements Proposition 4 with α/K
per class; the omitted part of class k is computed from the least-squares projection of the largest
class on class k over the design, and its ∥ · ∥ν,W norm is maximised over the largest-class region.
The invisible defect of Proposition 3 is evaluated with H the largest class, µN replaced by ν (the
designs are uniform on the target box) and γ = 0.5.

4.5   Baselines
Fixed threshold : declare the span of all generalised eigenvectors of (C(β̂), D(β̂)) whose plug-in
defect is at most τ ; sweeping τ traces the whole family, including τ = δ. Eigengap: declare the d
maximising ρ(d+1) /ρ(d) over d ≥ 1; this rule cannot express “no symmetry”, which is one reason its
error rate is high. Weyl perturbation certificate: bound λk (C(β ⋆ )) ≤ λk (C(β̂)) + ∥C(β ⋆ ) − C(β̂)∥
with the perturbation bounded by 2∥A(β̂)∥ ∥∆A∥F + ∥∆A∥2F , ∥∆A∥F maximised over the same
region, and divide by a lower bound on λmin (D); this is the natural singular-value perturbation
answer [10, 25, 65], valid and loose because it spends one global perturbation budget on every
direction. Rank test: for each d, test H0 : the d smallest plug-in directions span an exact nullspace,
using the squared norm of the restricted bracket referred to a Welch–Satterthwaite approximation
of its null distribution, and report the largest d not rejected, a Wald-type rank test in the spirit of
Robin and Smith [54] and Kleibergen and Paap [40]. Split significance test: select the smallest-defect
direction on one half of the data, test H0 : exact symmetry on the other half by the same statistic,
and declare a symmetry when the test fails to reject. Parametric bootstrap: draw β ∗ ∼ N (β̂, Σ̂) 400
times and report the α and 1 − α quantiles of ρ(θ; β ∗ ) [28].

4.6   Benchmarks, metrics and Monte Carlo uncertainty
Ten polynomial systems on R2 and R3 serve as the main benchmarks: four with a nonzero symmetry
algebra inside the affine class (a Hopf normal form, a planar rotationally symmetric linear system,
the Euler equations of a symmetric top, and a radial flow onto the sphere with full so(3)) and
six with none (Van der Pol, Duffing, Lotka–Volterra, Sel’kov, an asymmetric rigid body, Lorenz).
Every system has rational coefficients, so its exact symmetry algebra is the exact rational nullspace
of the bracket map, computed with symbolic arithmetic, and the true defect of any generator
follows in closed form from monomial moments; the algebra dimensions are cross-checked against an
independent characterisation (for linear systems, the dimension of the commutant of the coefficient
matrix). For the dictionary families the true algebra is the numerical nullspace of the exact bracket
map, with a gap of more than ten orders of magnitude between its zero and nonzero singular values.
    False certification is the event that the declared subspace contains a generator whose true defect
exceeds δ; because the declared object is a subspace, this is evaluated as a generalised eigenvalue of
the true forms restricted to it, not by sampling directions. Detection is the event that the declared
dimension is at least the true one, and recovery the event that, in addition, the declared subspace
contains the true algebra up to a largest principal angle of 10◦ in the L2 (ν) geometry of generator
fields. Unless stated otherwise δ = α = 0.05.

4.7   Ocean drifter data
Hourly positions and velocities are from the Global Drifter Program product of Elipot et al. [29],
version 2.01 [30], obtained from the NOAA AOML ERDDAP server on 26 October 2026 for 30–45◦ N,
180–140◦ W and 1 June to 30 September of 2016–2019. We kept rows recorded before the loss of the
drogue and with interpolation gaps of at most six hours, split each drifter’s record into contiguous
hourly segments of at least 150 hours, removed a centred running mean (49 hours unless stated) to
isolate the near-inertial band, and took central differences for the acceleration, dividing by the local
Coriolis parameter so that time is measured in units of 1/f ; velocities are expressed in units of their
root-mean-square. Every step acts identically on both velocity components and commutes with
rotations, so the processing can neither create nor remove rotation invariance. The model classes
are polynomials of degree 1 to 3 in the velocity with affine candidate generators, the target measure
is the standard Gaussian in the normalised velocity (extrapolation factor 1.02 for the linear class),
and the regions are OLS, White, cluster-robust over drifters (31 clusters) and Newey–West within
segments. The processed subset is distributed with the code.

4.10    Software, reproducibility and compute
The implementation (Python, NumPy, SciPy and SymPy) and all experiment and figure scripts are
released together with the raw result files (Code availability). All experiments are deterministic
given the seeds recorded in the code. Every table body in the manuscript and the Supplementary
Information is written by a script directly from the result files, and every number in the running text
is asserted against those files by a checking script, which fails if any has drifted. A test suite verifies
the bracket against finite differences, the closed-form moments against Monte Carlo integration,
the defect against Monte Carlo evaluation of its defining integrals, the exact symmetry algebra
of linear systems against the dimension of the commutant, the certified trust-region bounds and
the sharp ratio bounds against brute-force search over the ball, the invariance of the defect under
changes of units with a transported metric, the dictionary construction against the closed-form one,
the coverage of the coefficient region and of the certificate against their nominal levels, and the
closure diagnostic on exact Lie algebras. One complete pass of the experiments costs X CPU-hours
as recorded by the experiment runner.

Supplementary Note 1: Invariances of a learned model
The same machinery applies where F is a learned scalar function f and ξ generates an invariance:
the Lie derivative becomes ∇f · ξ, and the defect becomes the root-mean-square cosine of the angle
between the model’s gradient and the generator, again a ratio of norms of linear images of the
coefficients. We fit degree-6 polynomial regressions, with features fixed in advance, to noisy samples
of three families of planar target: a rotation-invariant one, three that are invariant only up to a
controlled amount, and one that is not. We then ask which invariances of the target may be declared
(Supplementary Fig. S6). With the training inputs covering the disc, both the threshold rule and
SymCert recover the one-dimensional invariance once N is large enough, SymCert requiring
roughly 16 times more data at noise level 0.05. With the training inputs covering a 120◦ sector, the
threshold rule declares invariances that the target does not have, at rates up to 0.76, for both an
invariant and a non-invariant target; SymCert never does.
    This is a second and distinct coverage mechanism. In the main text the integral was extrapolated:
the Gram matrix computed on the data stood in for the one on the target domain, and κ measured
the gap. Here the integral is exact and the model is extrapolated: outside the sector the fit is
unconstrained, and it drifts toward functions that happen to look invariant. No diagnostic on the
states alone can see this. SymCert does, because the same lack of constraint that lets the fit drift
also inflates Σ̂ in exactly those directions, and the certificate is then too wide to clear any useful
tolerance. The region used here is the classical one, which is valid because the polynomial features
are fixed in advance; for features learned on the same data it would not be (main text, “Learned
features”).


Supplementary Note 2: A case study on real measurements
The Hudson’s Bay Company lynx and hare pelt records for 1900–1920 are the canonical real data for
two-dimensional predator–prey model discovery: 21 annual counts, from which central differences
give 19 usable state–derivative pairs. Fitting a quadratic vector field (Q = 12) and running the
pipeline with White’s sandwich
                         √       gives the plug-in defect spectrum (0.18, 0.33, 0.68, 1.17, 1.31, 1.40),
an extrapolation factor κκ′ = 58, and a smallest certified tolerance of 2.3, larger than the largest
value the defect can take. With nineteen observations, no symmetry claim of any kind is supported.
The plug-in spectrum nonetheless looks informative, and at a tolerance of 0.3, not an unreasonable
choice, a threshold rule would report a one-dimensional symmetry algebra. We present this as a
case study rather than as a validity claim: with 19 rows the sandwich region has no asymptotic
justification, and derivatives differenced from noisy annual counts put error in the regressors, the
departure the guarantee does not cover.

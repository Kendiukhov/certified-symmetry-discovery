\title{\bf Certified Symmetry Discovery:\\ Confidence Sets for Lie-Algebra Generators\\ from Noisy, Limited Data}
\author{Anonymous}
\date{}

\begin{abstract}
Continuous symmetries of a dynamical system, or invariances of a learned model,
can be found by linear algebra: a candidate generator is a symmetry exactly when
a certain linear operator annihilates it, so the symmetries are the nullspace of
that operator. With real data the operator is estimated, no singular value is
exactly zero, and a symmetry is declared when one of them is judged \emph{small}.
That judgement has no error control, and its logic runs backwards: declaring a
symmetry because a test failed to reject it controls the chance of \emph{missing}
a symmetry, not the chance of \emph{inventing} one. We recast symmetry discovery
as an equivalence test: a generator may be declared a $\delta$-approximate
symmetry only when the data \emph{rule out} a defect larger than $\delta$. We
give a procedure, \symcert, that does this in closed form. Two ingredients make
it work: a scale-free defect functional, so that $\delta$ means the same thing
across systems and candidate bases; and the observation that the defect is a
ratio of norms of linear images of the model coefficients, so a confidence
region for those coefficients propagates through it exactly, by trust-region
subproblems. The resulting bound holds \emph{simultaneously for every
generator}, so no correction for selection or multiplicity and no sample
splitting is needed, and we show splitting is in fact counterproductive here.
On ten benchmark systems whose symmetry algebras are known exactly, across $240$
noise and sample-size settings and $72{,}000$ replications, \symcert{} never
certifies a generator whose true defect exceeds the tolerance, while a fixed
singular-value threshold reaches $63\%$ at its worst setting, an eigengap
heuristic $100\%$, and a nullspace-rank test $100\%$. The same machinery yields
three further results: a computable factor that bounds how much incomplete
coverage of the state space can inflate a defect, which is infinite exactly when
the data lie on a single trajectory, and which correctly ruled out spurious
symmetry in every setting where it applied; a Le~Cam lower bound showing that
the certifiable tolerance must scale as $\sigma/\sqrt{N}$, a rate \symcert{}
attains within a constant factor; and the finding that the largest source of
false symmetry is the model class rather than noise, which biases the defect
\emph{upward}: sparse regression on a weakly symmetry-breaking system deletes
the term that breaks the symmetry and reports an exact one.
\end{abstract}
